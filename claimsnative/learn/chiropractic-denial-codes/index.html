Chiropractic denial codes, and what to do with each one.

Every denied line on your remittance carries a reason code. Some point to paperwork your office can fix today. Others point to a judgment only your provider can make. Sorting each one into the right pile decides who fixes it.

Read the group code first

A denial on your electronic remittance advice is a pair: a group code and a claim adjustment reason code, or CARC, such as CO-50. The group code says who carries the amount. CO means your practice does, and it is generally not billed to the patient; for a Medicare patient it never is. PR means the patient does. The reason code says why. Often a remittance advice remark code, or RARC, adds the detail you need to act on it. If those terms are new, start with how to read an ERA.

One check before you work any denial: make sure it is a denial. A claim the clearinghouse or payer refused before processing is a rejection, it never appears on the remittance, and it needs a different fix. The guide to rejected and denied claims explains the difference.

Two kinds of denial

Sort every denial into one of two piles before anyone touches it.

Administrative denials are paperwork: a wrong ID, a missing field, a missing authorization, a provider the payer has not finished enrolling. Your office can fix them without asking your provider anything.

Clinical denials question the care itself: was it necessary, were there too many visits, does the diagnosis support the service. The answer starts with what the signed note supports, and that call belongs to your provider.

Coding denials sit between the two. Your office can spot a missing modifier or a mismatched diagnosis. Whether the note supports a different code or modifier is your provider’s decision.

Clinical denials your provider owns

CodeWhat the payer is sayingCommon causeNext step
CO-50The payer does not consider the service medically necessaryThe note does not show active treatment: no measurable goals, no progress, care that reads as maintenanceYour provider reviews the note. If it supports the care, appeal with the records. If it does not, accept the denial.
CO-151The information submitted does not support this many services, or this frequencyMore visits than the payer’s policy expects for the condition, without a documented reasonSend the treatment plan and progress notes if they justify the frequency.
CO-167The diagnosis is not coveredA diagnosis the plan’s chiropractic policy does not accept as primaryYour provider confirms the diagnosis the note supports.

One rule holds for every clinical denial: a signed note is never changed to win an appeal. Your provider decides what the note supports, and the appeal says what the note says.

Medicare and the AT modifier

Medicare pays for chiropractic manipulation only as active, corrective treatment of a subluxation, billed with CMT codes 98940, 98941 or 98942. Each CMT line for active treatment carries the AT modifier. Maintenance therapy is not considered medically reasonable or necessary. A line without the AT modifier is treated as maintenance and denied, and a line with it still needs a note that supports active treatment.

When your provider expects Medicare to deny a visit as maintenance, the patient can sign an Advance Beneficiary Notice before the visit, and the claim carries the GA modifier. If Medicare then denies the visit, the patient owes it. Without a valid notice, your practice carries the denial. Some commercial plans follow similar rules for active treatment; check each payer’s chiropractic policy.

Correct, reconsider or appeal

Once you know the reason, pick the route that fits it:

  • Corrected claim. You billed it wrong: a missing modifier, a wrong diagnosis, a missing field. For commercial payers, send a replacement claim that cites the payer’s claim control number from the remittance; sent as a new claim, it earns a duplicate denial. Medicare Part B takes no replacement claims, so a clerical error there is fixed through a reopening.
  • Reconsideration. You billed it right and the payer applied its rule wrong, such as an allowed amount below your contract rate. Many payers offer a first-level review before a formal appeal.
  • Appeal. A formal request with records, usually for clinical denials. Each payer sets its appeal deadline in its provider manual or your contract. Put that date on the claim the day the denial arrives.

Track the pattern, not only the claim

A denial you fix once is a claim. The same denial from the same payer three times is a rule your claims do not follow yet. Once a month, count denials by payer and reason code. Each repeat earns a check before filing: verify remaining visits for the plan that keeps returning 119, confirm the authorization for the plan that keeps returning 197, check the modifier for the plan that keeps returning 4. Fewer denials come back when the check moves ahead of the claim.

How Claims Native works denials

We sort every denial into paperwork we fix ourselves and questions only your provider can answer. A clinical question goes to your provider once, with the note and the payer’s exact words; your provider decides what the note says. A person reviews every reconsideration, appeal and payer dispute before it goes out. Then we record what the payer needed, so the next claim goes out the way that payer wants it.

How to switch billers without losing claims in progress.

The risk in changing billers is not the new one. It is the claims in flight on the day you switch: sent but unanswered, denied but unworked, paid but unposted. Decide who owns each one before the cutover, and keep the two running side by side until the numbers agree.

Read your contract before you give notice

Four clauses decide how hard the switch will be. Find them before you tell your current biller anything.

  • Notice period and renewal. How many days of notice, and whether the contract renews on its own if you miss the window.
  • Run-out. Who works claims with dates of service before the end date, for how long, and what the biller charges on payments that arrive after you leave.
  • Records. What the biller hands back, in what format and by when: claims, remittances, denial history, notes on open claims.
  • The business associate agreement. HIPAA requires it to say that when the arrangement ends, the biller returns or destroys your patients’ information where that is feasible. Ask for your full export, in writing, before that clock starts.

If the contract is silent on run-out or records, agree on both in writing before notice, while your current biller still wants to keep you.

Accounts the practice must own

A switch stalls when the accounts your billing depends on belong to the biller. Before notice, make sure each of these is in the practice’s name, with the biller added as a user you can remove:

  • The clearinghouse account your claims go out through and your remittances come back to.
  • Payer portal logins for eligibility, claim status and appeals.
  • Each provider’s credentialing profile. Many health plans read the CAQH profile during credentialing. The provider maintains it, chooses which plans can see it and must re-attest it every 120 days, or it expires. CAQH renamed itself DataSpring in 2026; profiles and logins carried over.
  • The bank account payer payments land in. Electronic payments should go straight to the practice’s account. If any payer pays the biller’s account instead, change that first; it takes the longest.
  • Your EHR’s administrator login. The biller’s user should be one you can switch off.

Take inventory on the cutover date

Pick a cutover date and ask your current biller for a list of every open claim as of that date, as a file, with its status and its filing deadline. Then agree on the totals. The list sorts into six piles:

StatusWhat it meansWhy it gets lost
Not yet sentVisits with no claimNobody owns them between the two billers. The filing clock is already running.
RejectedClaims the clearinghouse or payer refused before processingThey sit in the old clearinghouse’s reports, which the new biller cannot see. See rejected vs denied claims.
Sent, no answerAccepted by the payer but not yet paid or deniedThe remittance may arrive at the old clearinghouse after you leave.
Denied, not workedDenials with no correction or appeal yetAppeal deadlines keep running during the switch.
Paid, not postedPayments received but not recorded in your EHRPatient balances are wrong until they post.
Credit balancesOverpayments by a payer or a patientSomeone has to refund them, and the clock on refunds does not stop for a switch.

Decide who owns each old claim

Draw the line by date of service, and write it down. Three arrangements are common:

  • The old biller runs out its claims. It keeps working claims with dates of service before the cutover for an agreed period, and is paid on what it collects. The new biller files everything after the cutover.
  • The new biller takes the old claims. Cleaner for you, but often priced separately, because old claims take more work per dollar.
  • Your office works them. Realistic only for a short list.

Whichever you choose, set a date to review what is left, and decide then what to appeal, what to bill and what to write off.

Move remittances and payments

Claims can move to a new biller in a day. Remittances and payments cannot.

Remittances. Each payer sends remittances to the receiver you enrolled, and changing it takes a new ERA enrollment with each payer. Medicare contractors limit it to one receiver: Novitas, for instance, allows one ERA receiver per PTAN for Part B, and changes go through the contractor’s EDI enrollment form. Until each payer’s enrollment changes, remittances keep arriving where they arrived before, so keep access to the old clearinghouse, or have the old biller forward every remittance, until the last payer has switched.

Payments. If payer payments already land in the practice’s account, nothing changes, because electronic payment enrollment follows your bank account, not your biller. If any payer pays an account the biller controls, change that enrollment first: for Medicare with the CMS-588 form, which requires the practice to have sole control of the account, and for commercial payers through each payer’s own enrollment process.

Run both side by side

The safest switch has an overlap. For a few weeks, the new biller prepares and checks every claim from your visits while the current biller keeps filing. Each week you compare: which claims each one would send, what each one found, where they differ. When the two agree, and the new biller’s enrollment is complete, you switch. Cash keeps arriving the whole time, because the current biller never stopped.

The overlap also tells you something no proposal can: whether the new biller finds problems the old one missed. Use the five numbers to compare the months before and after.

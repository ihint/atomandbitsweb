How I work

Let’s understand what we’re asking of people.

A plan becomes real when someone sits down to use it, whether that’s a clinic’s front desk or a credit union’s team at month-end close. I get curious about those people before we commit. What are you trying to achieve, and what will it ask of them?

I’ll bring a point of view, ask a lot of questions, and say the uncomfortable thing out loud. Then I leave room for your team to change my mind, and I’m grateful when they do.

Carving a spoon has taught me something I already knew from building software: you follow the grain instead of fighting it. The best systems are built around how people already work, not the other way around.

Learning from your team

The people who do the job know the most.

I prepare before we meet: how your company earns money, who it serves, and what’s already underway. That way our time together goes to the choices in front of us.

Then I sit with the people who handle those tasks every day. A document describes a process. The person handling the exceptions knows how it really goes, and often has the best idea in the room. I want both before we decide what to change.

One to One Health

When I joined One to One Health, patients valued the care and clinicians enjoyed delivering it. New clients were good news, and each one asked more of a team running on manual processes, spreadsheets and the know-how of a few people.

Eligibility and onboarding belonged in our product plan alongside clinical care. I set that direction for the full product suite and hired the founding engineers. Building that suite would take time, and I wanted clinical and operations staff to get something useful right away.

So I chose clinical documentation as a place to start. I designed and developed the initial AI infrastructure to prepare clinical notes for provider review. Charting time fell from 22 to 17.5 minutes across all encounters, which gave clinicians back 4.5 minutes on an average encounter, while clinician and patient satisfaction were maintained or improved.

Reaching agreement

Be clear about what we mean.

Words matter. If a service needs to be faster, who is waiting? I ask what they are waiting for and what a useful improvement would look like. Those answers shape what we agree to build.

Questions give people room to challenge an assumption, add a missing detail, or tell me a proposal would make their day harder. That’s a gift, and I listen for it. A good agreement holds what we’ve learned together.

TPN.health

At TPN, we were discussing invoicing payers directly, and I asked, “What exactly are we trying to achieve here?” Were we trying to improve throughput? Invoicing? Payouts? Working through the answer together turned a conversation about invoices into a plan for a claims filing and revenue capture platform. I love that turn, when someone supplies a missing detail and a team suddenly sees a more useful thing to build.

Taking responsibility

Stay for the good part.

Once we agree on what’s worth doing, I own the part I lead. That can mean shaping the product, making a technical call, hiring the team, or working through terms with a customer. We agree up front on what I own and the support it needs.

Before we begin, we agree on how to judge the result. At One to One Health, the useful outcome was less charting time with clinician and patient satisfaction maintained or improved. In an agent pilot, we need to count the effort of reviewing the output as well as the tasks software takes on.

If a change saves time for one person but adds work for someone else, I count that too. We need to know whether it actually delivers value.

Where the joy is

Building this should be fun.

My favorite moments are small ones. At TheraMatch, I wanted to know what happened after a recommendation, so we measured whether each person was still with their first therapist a month later. When PerfectServe cut provider onboarding from five to seven days to under an hour, providers could start using the product within the hour instead of waiting most of a week. One question about invoices at TPN changed what we built. Someone notices a detail, someone else asks why, and a team sees something better to build. I look forward to that conversation every time.

Chattanooga is home. My wife and I are raising three children here, along with three dogs and ten chickens, and I serve on the board of The Mission Chattanooga. You can take a hard question seriously and still enjoy working it out with people you like and trust. I try to work that way close to home and with teams far from it.

The best part is usually the moment someone on the team says, “Oh, that’s so much easier.” That’s what I’m working toward.

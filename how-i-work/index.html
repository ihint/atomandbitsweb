How I work

I want us to understand what we’re asking of people.

A plan can look quite reasonable until you follow it into someone’s working day. I want to understand both: what the business needs to achieve, and what will have to happen for the people asked to deliver it.

That takes preparation and curiosity. It also takes a willingness to let what we learn change what we thought we should do.

Understanding the work

Follow the plan into the working day.

Before we talk, I do the research I can. I want to understand how the business earns its money, who it serves, and what is already underway. Preparation gives me a point of view to bring to the conversation and makes better use of the time people give us.

Then I want to hear from the people who know the work. A document can describe how a process is meant to run. The person handling an exception can explain what happens when it does not. Both matter when we decide what to build.

At One to One Health, the opportunity was to bring valued care to more people. That required a plan for getting new clients’ patients into care, alongside an early improvement the clinical and operations teams could benefit from while we built the wider product suite.

Reaching agreement

Be clear about what we mean.

I place a great deal of importance on language. When someone says a service needs to be faster, I want to understand who is waiting, what they are waiting for, and what a useful improvement would look like. The answer helps us agree on the work we are actually asking a team to do.

Questions also give people a way to shape that answer. I want someone to be able to challenge an assumption, supply the detail we missed, or explain why a proposal would make their work harder. That is how we build an understanding people can act on.

At TPN, asking what payer invoicing was meant to achieve helped us define an internal claims filing and revenue capture platform. The question mattered because it changed the deliverable we could agree on.

Taking responsibility

Stay with the work long enough to see what changes.

Once we understand what is worth doing, I’m comfortable taking responsibility for the work. That can mean shaping the product, making a technical decision, hiring the team, or working through the commercial terms with a customer. We agree on what I will own and what support the work needs.

I also want us to know how we will judge the result. At One to One Health, the useful outcome was less charting time with clinician and patient satisfaction maintained or improved. In an agent pilot, we need to count the effort of reviewing the output as well as the work the software takes on.

The measures help us learn. If a change makes one part of the process easier but gives someone else more work, we need to see that and decide what to do about it.

The company we keep

There should be something to enjoy along the way.

I most enjoy working with curious, kind, thoughtful people. I care about the quality of our thinking and the way we treat one another while we work. We can take a difficult question seriously and still find joy in exploring it together.

There is a pleasure in discovering something useful with people who each see a different part of the problem. I want that understanding to become something we can put to work, and something the people involved can feel good about building.

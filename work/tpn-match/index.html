Atom & Bits / Ian Harman / TPN Match

TPN Match · A question that changed the plan

We started by talking
about invoices.

Ian Harman · VP, Provider Networks & Technology · TPN.health · February 2025 to August 2026

Asking what we wanted to achieve led us to a broader product: an internal claims filing system and a platform to manage revenue capture.

The question I asked

“What exactly are we trying to achieve here?”

Before deciding how to invoice payers, I wanted us to be clear about the result we were after.

The conversation

First, we had to name the goal.

The discussion at TPN centered on invoicing payers directly. I asked what we were trying to achieve. Were we trying to improve throughput? Invoicing? Payouts? What, exactly, were we trying to do?

That question helped us work out that we needed an internal claims filing system and a platform to manage revenue capture. Once we understood the goal, we could think more clearly about the product it called for.

For TPN Match, the broader job connected provider participation, payer terms, eligibility, healthcare EDI, claim repricing, remittance, and payment. Providers needed clear referrals and predictable payment. Payer and TPA partners needed accurate terms and reliable transactions. Care navigators needed to understand what was happening to the person seeking care.

These were parts of the same business problem. We had to build a product that could connect them.

My part in the build

The question was a beginning. Then we had a system to build.

I led product and platform strategy as TPN Match expanded from matching into network, claims, and payment operations. My role also covered technical sales, integrations, and technology relationships with payer and TPA partners.

That meant working through what the business needed alongside the technical choices that would make it possible. I also led SOC 2 security and privacy audit work, HIPAA compliance, and the HIPAA attestation milestone.

We focused on a complete payment path before expanding the roadmap. The team was learning from live claims while building the system, so each decision had to hold up in the actual work.

Choosing the tools

AI had a useful role. So did fixed rules and human judgment.

A knowledge graph organized claims and claim types from different sources. A machine-learning model used that information to estimate negotiated rates and reached production during my tenure. Contracted claim repricing used fixed rules based on CPT code, location, and effective terms.

AI also handled early inbound messages and gathered assessment details before navigator review. People remained responsible for the care and operating decisions that needed their judgment.

We kept the transaction states distinct. A submitted claim, an accepted claim, a remittance, and a payment told us different things. Denials and failures also needed their own records. The product had to preserve those differences so people could see what had happened and what still needed attention.

The aim was to absorb more work without adding staff at the same rate. Internal tools helped parse claims, build the knowledge base, support negotiated-rate learning, and prepare inbound conversations.

What the team delivered

A live claims and payment system.

From January through June 2026, the product processed more than $1 million in claims volume. The contracted base covered 120 TPAs and about 1.5 million eligible lives.

Across about 4,000 claims from April through June 2026, 99.59% passed repricing on the first attempt. The negotiated-rate model reached production, and the system connected matching to the work required for claims and payment.

Those results took a team. My contribution was to help define what we needed to build, lead the product and technology choices, and connect them to the payer relationships and operations that made the product useful.

About this account and these results

The opening story is my recollection of the conversation. The figures are my reported results from my work at TPN. The $1 million measure is processed claims volume from January through June 2026, not company revenue or collected cash. The 99.59% measure is first-pass repricing, not a collection rate. These results reflect the team’s work under my product and technology leadership.

Why I remember it

A better understanding changed the product.

That conversation is a recent example of why I ask questions. Understanding the goal helped us agree on a more useful system to build. It brought the business, product, and technology discussion together.

If you’re working through a question about what your team should build, I’d enjoy thinking it through with you.

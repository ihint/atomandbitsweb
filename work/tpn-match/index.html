Atom & Bits / Ian Harman / TPN Match

Case study · Platform · Claims · AI

I helped turn TPN Match into claims and payment operations.

Ian Harman · VP, Provider Networks & Technology · TPN.health · February 2025 to August 2026

A match only matters if the network can support it and the provider can get paid. Ian's scope joined product strategy, platform strategy, payer technology, technical integrations, technical sales, claims, payment, and AI-assisted operations.

The problem

The product could not stop at finding a provider.

TPN Match needed to connect a member's search for care to provider participation, payer terms, eligibility context, healthcare EDI, claim repricing, remittance, and payment. Each step had its own system, owner, rules, and failure states.

The operating problem

Providers needed clear referrals and predictable payment. TPAs and payer partners needed accurate terms and reliable transactions. Care navigators needed tools that reduced work without hiding the member's status or taking away human judgment.

The platform problem

The team had to build a full product while learning from live claims. It needed enough automation to avoid adding headcount, but not at the cost of explainability, audit, or control over money.

What Ian owned

The choices that connected product, technology, payers, and operations.

01 / Product

Led product strategy, platform strategy, roadmap choices, and the move from matching into a wider network, claims, and payment system.

02 / Market

03 / Trust

Led SOC 2 security and privacy audit work, HIPAA compliance, and the HIPAA attestation milestone.

The hard decisions

Use AI to learn from messy inputs. Use fixed rules to move money.

AI boundary

Separate interpretation from authority.

A knowledge graph organized claims and claim types from different sources. A production machine-learning model used that data to estimate negotiated rates. Contracted repricing stayed deterministic by CPT code, location, and effective terms.

Human boundary

Use AI to reduce work without hiding high-impact choices.

AI handled early inbound messages and gathered assessment details before navigator review. People stayed responsible for the care and operating decisions that required judgment.

Transaction boundary

Keep each claim state true.

Submitted, accepted, remitted, paid, denied, and failed were different states. The system needed to record each one and preserve the evidence behind it.

Speed boundary

Ship an end-to-end payment path before expanding the roadmap.

The team focused on the smallest complete transaction. That choice connected the steps needed for payment before the team expanded the workflow.

What changed

The team moved from a matching product to a live claims and payment system.

From January through June 2026, the product handled more than $1 million in total claims volume across submission, remittance, and payment. The contracted base covered 120 TPAs and about 1.5 million eligible lives.

Quality at scale

Across about 4,000 claims in April, May, and June 2026, 99.59% passed repricing on the first attempt. The negotiated-rate ML model reached production during Ian’s tenure.

Capacity without the same hiring curve

Internal AI tools parsed claims, built the knowledge base, supported negotiated-rate learning, and handled early inbound conversations. The goal was direct: absorb more work without adding the same amount of staff.

What the work proves

Ian can choose the right boundary between AI, rules, software, and people.

The work joins payer strategy, architecture, integrations, production code, claims operations, security, sales, and measurement. It also shows restraint: the system used AI where the input was uncertain and fixed logic where the outcome had to be exact.

Evidence and limits

These figures are Ian’s reported results from his work at TPN. The $1 million figure is processed claims volume from January through June 2026. It is not company revenue or a claim of $1 million in collected cash. The results reflect team work under Ian's product and technology leadership.

The AI lesson

Not every hard workflow needs a model.

The useful question is where AI adds judgment, where software should stay deterministic, and where the team should fix the work before it builds.
